AI tools are changing how scientists write and review software. More
importantly to us, they are also changing the dynamics among the
people doing that work. The Alfred P. Sloan Foundation has funded
pyOpenSci to understand those changes and build practical resources to
help our community navigate them.

That matters to us because pyOpenSci supports the people who build and
maintain the scientific Python software research depends on:
contributors learning to write better software, maintainers reviewing
that work, and the trust between them that makes open source possible.

## What we've been hearing

Over the past six months, pyOpenSci has led this conversation in many
different spaces — our AI tool adoption session at [UN Open Source
Week](/blog/un-open-source-week-2026-ai-community.html), our conversations about [generative
AI at PyCon US](/blog/pycon-us-2026-generative-ai-open-source.html),
birds of a feather sessions at SciPy 2026, focus group sessions at
EuroPython, and the day-to-day of our peer review process and mentored
sprints, where we see how these tools land on people who are still
learning.

Three things stood out in these sessions:

1. **The strain on human systems is real.** People and their networks that sustain open
   source are feeling the weight of AI tool adoption. Maintainers, in
   particular, miss the natural human interactions that used to fuel
   open source.
2. **The conversation around AI tool use can be polarizing.** Polarization
   pushes certain voices to the top and leaves people unsure where
   they stand in a community.
3. **Many people find these tools genuinely useful** in specific,
   well-defined ways. pyOpenSci data collected from peer review shows that most are using AI tools daily in their work.

## What we're going to do

While many projects have developed AI policies on their own, what
struck us across all of this is that no one has systematically
explored what good AI tool use actually looks like in scientific open
source, or how it's affecting the community.

pyOpenSci wants to understand what's really happening, and then build
some useful resources grounded in what we learn from the community.

Our work has two parts. **First, we're listening at scale.** We'll run a
survey and a series of focus groups with maintainers, contributors,
and research software engineers, and we'll review the AI policies
projects have already written to see what's working and where the gaps
are.

**Second, we'll build from what we learn.** We're developing practical
guidance for contributors on working with these tools deliberately:
designing alongside the tool rather than just accepting its output,
asking it questions, iterating, and using it to learn concepts you
didn't know before. All of it starts from the software design
principles that make good software good in the first place —
modularity, legibility, scoping work before you write it. Those
principles don't change when the tools do, which is what makes them
worth anchoring to.

We're also developing a resource to help maintainers set clear
expectations for AI-assisted contributions without having to write a
policy from scratch.

Running through both is a commitment to critical, responsible
adoption. Using these tools effectively means reckoning with what they cost —
licensing questions, environmental impact, and the impact on the
people and communities doing this work. Those tradeoffs belong in guiding the
conversation, not as a footnote to it.

## Why pyOpenSci

We've been teaching people to build better scientific software for
years, and that teaching has always been grounded in research on how
researchers actually learn. That combination matters here: these
questions are as much about learning and behavior as they are about
software.

We also have reach into the places where this is playing out. Our peer
review process and mentored sprints show us how these tools affect
people who are still building their skills. Our university
partnerships connect us to researchers and research software
engineers. And our community spans industry, academia, core Python,
and the scientific Python ecosystem, which means we can bring the
maintainers of widely used tools into the same conversation as the
people just getting started.

## Get involved

We succeed together. This only works if it reflects what's actually happening across the
community, so we want to hear from you. There will be a survey, focus
groups, and an open review period where you can help shape what we
build. Everything we produce will be openly licensed and free to
adapt. [LINK: sign up / follow along]

There's something hopeful in all of this, and we've seen it firsthand.
Even with the strain AI tools are placing on open source, what works
is making space for people to connect and work together — especially
volunteers. We've seen it in our peer review process, and in the work
our Community and Research Lead, Shauna McKeon-Gordon, has done to
strengthen how we bring new reviewers in. The human parts of this are
still the parts we have the most control over.
