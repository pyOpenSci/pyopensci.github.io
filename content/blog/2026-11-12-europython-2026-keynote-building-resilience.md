Many of us are tired right now. AI tools are changing how we write,
review, and maintain software, and the pace of change is fast. We are also seeing
new strains that AI tools create in communication as it's hard to sometimes tell whether an account is a human or AI.

In July our executive director Leah Wasser gave a keynote at EuroPython in Kraków on what ultrarunning
taught her about staying resilient through times like this. You can check it out below
or read on to get the key takeaways.

Download the slides: [EuroPython 2026 Keynote - Building Resilience When Things Feel Hard](https://zenodo.org/records/21396814).

## Stress is stress, and noise makes it worse

Your body doesn't care whether stress comes from a hard run, a job
search, or a flood of AI news. It reads all of it the same way. What
makes it worse is **noise**: reactions to incomplete information. On Leah's
first 160 km run, she once froze in the dark, certain she was surrounded by
mountain lions. They were actually sleeping cows. One scare is survivable. The
same jolt over and over wears you down.

{{< figure src="images/blog/2026/europython-2026-cow-mountain-lion.jpg" alt="A black-and-white cow with a mountain lion's face and long curved horns stands on a rocky ridge." caption="An AI-generated image showing a mountain lion disguised as a cow with spots." >}}

## Resilience is built, not found

Training works by adding small stresses and then resting. Your body
adapts, and you get stronger. Stress, rest, adapt, resilience. The
catch with AI is that none of us trained for it. The course is
undefined, and nobody has marked the aid stations. That's hard, and it
is still something people can do.

## See through the noise

Not every signal deserves your attention. Part of resilience is
deciding which ones to listen to and which to let go. Sometimes that
means staying the course when everything says to quit. Fires nearly
kept me off a trail I'd trained eight months for, and pushing through
that noise gave me one of the best experiences of my life. Other times
the noise points you somewhere new. Years of doubt during my PhD pushed
me out of academia and toward the work I do now.

{{< figure src="images/blog/2026/europython-2026-we-will-find-your-way.jpg" alt="A snowy mountain ridge rises above a sea of clouds. Across the sky, You is crossed out and the words read We will find your way." caption="We will find your way." >}}

If you feel redirected right now, ask where the redirection is
actually taking you. You may not be lost. You may be on the trail.

## You can't do it alone

Support systems are what make hard things possible. On long runs,
they're aid stations: someone with food, dry clothes, and, on one very
memorable day, a Coke. In open source and in research, they're mentors,
friends, and communities that make room for newcomers. These people
are often the difference between someone staying in a field and
leaving it.

<!-- IMAGE: Lachs, the cat of an open source friend who has been a
support system. Caption: "Support comes from unexpected places." -->

Human connection matters more in the age of AI, because the work is
faster and the social part is easier to skip. A conference is a good
start. The next step is a smaller circle you can reach after you go
home: colleagues, a local group, people who know your work and tell
you the truth.

## One step at a time

Big goals feel impossible when you stare at the finish. On a long
trail, my feet blistered, my brain said stop, and I was ready to quit.
Slowing down and focusing on the next step changed everything. I
didn't know how I would finish. I only needed to know I could keep
going.

[pyOpenSci](https://www.pyopensci.org/) grew this way too. It started
with a few people meeting, then a few more, and years later it's a
global community of contributors and maintainers.

## Choose on purpose, and rest

You can adopt AI tools when they're genuinely useful, or leave them
alone. What matters is noticing how that choice affects you and the
people around you. And take breaks. Adaptation happens during rest,
and skipping it leads to burnout.

<!-- IMAGE: a rest-day photo, such as the salt mines day with Mike. -->

## Know your north star

When the noise gets loud, a clear goal helps you filter it. At
pyOpenSci, ours is protecting our volunteers and helping researchers
build better research software so science can move faster. What is
yours? Protecting a maintainer team, carrying a project forward,
building something new, or chasing a question that matters?
