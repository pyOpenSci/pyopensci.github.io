Many of us are tired right now. AI tools are changing how we write,
review, and maintain software, and the pace of change is fast. We are also
seeing new strains in communication, as it can be hard to tell whether an
account belongs to a human or an AI.

In July, our executive director, Leah Wasser, gave a keynote at EuroPython in
Kraków on what ultrarunning taught her about staying resilient through times
like this. Watch below or read on for the key takeaways, and for why this
matters to the community we support at pyOpenSci.

Download the slides: [EuroPython 2026 Keynote - Building Resilience When Things Feel Hard](https://zenodo.org/records/21396814).

## Stress is stress, and noise makes it worse

Your body doesn't care whether stress comes from a hard run, a job search, or
a flood of new pull requests for AI fueled bots.
Your body reads all the noise the same way. What makes it harder is
**noise**: reactions to incomplete information. For example on GitHub, it's
hard to tell whether an account is a human or AI. On Leah's first 160 km run,
she once froze in the dark, certain she was surrounded by mountain lions.
They were actually sleeping cows. One scare is survivable. The same jolt over
and over wears us all down down and becomes increasingly harder to recover from.

Maintainers are being asked to react to AI claims they can't verify. At pyOpenSci, we are
collecting data to try to better understand what is happening int he community and
what we can do about this together. So we can tell the cows from the mountain lions and act (no re-act) accordingly.

## Resilience is built, not found

When you train for hard things, you intentionally stress your body over and
over, in doses it can recover from. Rest is when you recover and get
stronger. Stress, rest, adapt, resilience.

The catch with AI is that none of us trained for it. The course is
undefined, nobody has marked the aid stations, and it's hard to find space to
rest and recover. That's hard, and it is still something we can do. At
pyOpenSci, we are building guidelines for using AI tools well, grounded in
data on how maintainers and contributors adopt them. We see those guardrails
as aid stations.

## See through the noise

Not every signal needs your full attention. Part of resilience is
deciding which signals to listen to and which to let go. Sometimes that
means staying the course when everything says to quit. Fires nearly
kept Leah off a trail she had trained eight months for, and pushing through
that noise gave her one of the best experiences of her life. Other times
the noise points you somewhere new. Years of doubt during her PhD pushed
her out of academia and toward the work that became pyOpenSci.

{{< figure src="images/blog/2026/europython-2026-we-will-find-your-way.jpg" alt="A snowy mountain ridge rises above a sea of clouds. Across the sky, You is crossed out and the words read We will find your way." caption="We will find your way." >}}

If you feel redirected right now, consider where the redirection is
actually taking you. You may not be lost. You may actually be on the trail.

## You can't do it alone

Support systems are what make hard things possible. On long runs,
they're aid stations: someone with food, dry clothes, and, on one very
memorable day, a Coke. In open source and in research, they're mentors,
friends, and communities that make room for newcomers. These people
are often the difference between someone staying in a field and
leaving it. At pyOpenSci, peer review, sprints, and our community are
that support.

Human connection matters more in the age of AI, because the work is
faster and the social part is easier to skip. A conference is a good
start. The next step is a smaller circle you can reach after you go
home: colleagues, a local group, people who know your work and tell
you the truth.

## Take one step at a time and don't worry about the finish line

Big goals feel impossible when you stare at the finish. On a long
trail, Leah's feet blistered, her brain said stop, and she was ready to quit.
Slowing down and focusing on the next step changed everything. She
didn't know how she would finish. She only needed to know she could keep
going.

[pyOpenSci](https://www.pyopensci.org/) grew this way too. It started
with a few people meeting, then a few more, and years later it's a
global community of contributors and maintainers.

## Choose on purpose, and rest

You can adopt AI tools when they're genuinely useful, or leave them
alone. What matters is noticing how that choice affects you and the
people around you. And take breaks. Adaptation happens during rest,
and skipping it leads to burnout.

## Know your north star

When the noise gets loud, a clear goal helps you filter it. At
pyOpenSci, ours is protecting our volunteers and helping researchers
build better research software so science can move faster. What is
yours? Protecting a maintainer team, carrying a project forward,
building something new, or chasing a question that matters?
